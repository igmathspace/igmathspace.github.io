📐 2. Structure of an LPP
Four components

Every linear programming model has four components:

  1. Decision variables
  2. Objective function
  3. Constraints
  4. Non-negativity restrictions
🔢 3. Decision Variables

Decision variables are the quantities whose values are to be determined by solving the LPP. They are denoted by \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\) and represent the actual decisions to be taken — e.g. the number of units of each product to produce, the quantity of each raw material to use, the number of units of each food to buy.

The variables must be interrelated and they must have limited supply of resources behind them; otherwise the model becomes degenerate.

🎯 4. Objective Function

The objective function is a linear expression of the decision variables that measures the effectiveness of the decision — typically profit, revenue, cost, loss, production capacity. It is written as

\[ Z = c_1 x_1 + c_2 x_2 + \cdots + c_n x_n \]

and is to be maximised (for profit/revenue) or minimised (for cost/loss). The constants \(c_j\) are called the objective function coefficients.

🔗 5. Constraints

Constraints are linear restrictions on the decision variables, arising either from limitations on resources (availability of raw material, machine hours, money) or from requirements (minimum production, minimum nutrient intake). They are written as linear equations or inequalities:

\[ a_{i1} x_1 + a_{i2} x_2 + \cdots + a_{in} x_n \;(\le,\;=,\;\ge)\; b_i,\quad i = 1, 2, \dots, m. \]

The constants \(a_{ij}\) are the input-output coefficients (or activity coefficients), and \(b_i\) is the availability (right-hand side) of the \(i\)-th resource.

🚫 6. Non-Negativity Restriction

The decision variables must satisfy

\[ x_1 \ge 0,\; x_2 \ge 0,\; \dots,\; x_n \ge 0. \]

This is a physical condition: a negative number of units cannot be produced or a negative amount of food cannot be bought. In the model, the non-negativity conditions are handled separately from the other constraints and are therefore called non-negativity restrictions.

⚠️ CAUTION! Although mathematically the non-negativity conditions do not differ from other constraints, they are treated separately in the simplex method and in the standard form. Never omit them when formulating a model.

Problem 1. Identify the feasible region \(x + y \le 3,\; x \ge 0,\; y \ge 0\). CO3
✅ Step-by-step:
Step 1: The constraint \(x + y \le 3\) is the region below the line \(x + y = 3\), which passes through \((3, 0)\) and \((0, 3)\).
Step 2: The non-negativity \(x \ge 0\), \(y \ge 0\) restricts the region to the first quadrant.
Step 3: The intersection of these two regions is the triangle with vertices
\(O(0,0)\), \(A(3,0)\), \(B(0,3)\).
Step 4: The feasible region is the closed triangular region bounded by the lines \(x = 0\), \(y = 0\), and \(x + y = 3\) in the first quadrant.

✅ Feasible region = triangle with vertices \((0,0), (3,0), (0,3)\).

Problem 2. Find the minimum value of \(z = 5x - 2y\), subject to \(2x + 3y \ge 1,\; x, y \ge 0\). CO3
✅ Step-by-step (formulation-based answer):
Step 1: Identify decision variables: \(x, y\) (≥ 0).
Step 2: Objective function: minimise \(z = 5x - 2y\).
Step 3: Constraint: \(2x + 3y \ge 1\) (a \(\ge\)-type constraint indicating the minimum requirement).
Step 4: Non-negativity: \(x \ge 0\), \(y \ge 0\).
Step 5: The feasible region is the unbounded region above the line \(2x + 3y = 1\) in the first quadrant, with corner points \((1/2, 0)\) and \((0, 1/3)\).
Step 6: Evaluate \(z = 5x - 2y\) at these corners: \(z(1/2, 0) = 5/2\), \(z(0, 1/3) = -2/3\).
Step 7: Since the feasible region is unbounded, but the minimum is attained at the corner \((0, 1/3)\), the minimum value is \(-2/3\).

✅ \(x = 0,\; y = 1/3,\; z_{\min} = -2/3\).

Problem 3. Define a surplus variable with an example. CO3
✅ Answer:
A surplus variable is a non-negative variable that is subtracted from the left-hand side of a \(\ge\)-type constraint to convert it into an equation. Physically it represents the amount by which the left-hand side exceeds the right-hand side.
Example: The constraint \(2x_1 + 3x_2 \ge 10\) is converted to
\(2x_1 + 3x_2 - s = 10, \quad s \ge 0\),
where \(s\) is the surplus variable, representing the surplus of the left side over 10.

✅ A surplus variable is the "negative slack" used in \(\ge\)-type constraints.

📋 9. Quick Summary
  • Decision variables: quantities to be determined; denoted \(x_j\).
  • Objective function: linear expression to be maximised or minimised.
  • Constraints: linear restrictions, from resource availability or minimum requirements.
  • Non-negativity: \(x_j \ge 0\) — a physical reality, handled separately.
