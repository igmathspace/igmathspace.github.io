📌 Review: Separable ODE Method
A first-order ODE is called separable if it can be written in the form: \[ \frac{dy}{dx} = g(x)h(y) \quad \text{or} \quad M(x)\,dx + N(y)\,dy = 0 \] Solution Method: \[ \int \frac{dy}{h(y)} = \int g(x)\,dx + C \] Always introduce the constant of integration immediately after integration!
🔧 The Modeling Process
Three Steps of Modeling:
  1. Step 1: Setting up the model — Translate the physical situation into a mathematical expression (an ODE with initial condition).
  2. Step 2: Mathematical solution — Solve the ODE using appropriate methods (separation of variables, etc.).
  3. Step 3: Interpretation — Translate the mathematical result back into physical terms.
💡 Key Insight: Your computer (CAS) may help with Step 2, but Steps 1 and 3 are essentially your work!
🧪 Application 1: Mixing Problems
General Setup for Mixing Problems:
  • A tank contains a solution (e.g., salt in water) of volume V.
  • Brine (or other solution) flows in at a certain rate and concentration.
  • The mixture is kept uniform by stirring.
  • Solution flows out at a certain rate.
  • Balance Law: Rate of change = Inflow rate − Outflow rate
\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = \text{(Inflow concentration)} \times \text{(Inflow rate)} - \frac{y(t)}{V(t)} \times \text{(Outflow rate)} \]
📖 Example 1: Mixing Problem (Kreyszig Example 5, p. 14)

Problem: A tank contains 1000 gal of water with 100 lb of salt dissolved. Brine runs in at 10 gal/min, each gallon containing 5 lb of salt. The mixture is kept uniform. Brine runs out at 10 gal/min. Find the amount of salt in the tank at any time t.

Solution:
Step 1: Setting up the model.
Let y(t) = amount of salt in the tank at time t.
Inflow: 5 lb/gal × 10 gal/min = 50 lb/min
Outflow: 10 gal/min is 10/1000 = 1% of the tank, so outflow = 0.01y(t)
\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = 50 - 0.01y = -0.01(y - 5000) \] Initial condition: y(0) = 100

Step 2: Mathematical solution.
\[ \frac{dy}{y - 5000} = -0.01\,dt \] \[ \ln|y - 5000| = -0.01t + C \] \[ y - 5000 = ce^{-0.01t} \] Using y(0) = 100: \[ 100 - 5000 = c \Rightarrow c = -4900 \] \[ \boxed{y(t) = 5000 - 4900e^{-0.01t}} \] Step 3: Interpretation.
As t → ∞, y(t) → 5000 lb. This is the limiting salt content when the brine in the tank reaches the same concentration as the incoming brine (5 lb/gal × 1000 gal = 5000 lb). The solution shows exponential approach to equilibrium.
📖 Example 2: Mixing Problem – Fresh Water Inflow

Problem: A tank contains 400 gal of brine with 100 lb of salt dissolved. Fresh water runs in at 2 gal/min. The mixture, kept uniform, runs out at the same rate. How much salt will be in the tank after 1 hour?

Solution:
Let y(t) = amount of salt in the tank at time t.
Inflow: fresh water contains 0 lb of salt, so inflow = 0
Outflow: 2 gal/min out of 400 gal = 2/400 = 0.005 of the tank per minute
Outflow rate = 0.005y(t)

\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = -0.005y \] \[ \frac{dy}{y} = -0.005\,dt \] \[ \ln|y| = -0.005t + C \] \[ y = ce^{-0.005t} \] y(0) = 100 ⇒ c = 100
\[ y(t) = 100e^{-0.005t} \] At t = 60 min: \[ y(60) = 100e^{-0.3} = 100 \times 0.7408 = \boxed{74.08 \text{ lb}} \]
🌡️ Application 2: Newton's Law of Cooling
Newton's Law of Cooling:
The time rate of change of the temperature T of a body is proportional to the difference between T and the temperature of the surrounding medium Tₐ. \[ \frac{dT}{dt} = k(T - T_a) \] where k is a constant (k < 0 for cooling, k > 0 for heating).
💡 Golden Rule: If you cannot solve your problem, try to solve a simpler one!
📖 Example 3: Heating an Office Building (Kreyszig Example 6, p. 15)

Problem: Daytime temperature in an office is maintained at 70°F. Heating is shut off at 10 PM and turned on at 6 AM. At 2 AM, the temperature was 65°F. Outside temperature was 50°F at 10 PM and dropped to 40°F by 6 AM. What was the temperature when the heat was turned on at 6 AM?

Solution:
Step 1: Setting up the model.
Let T(t) = temperature inside the building.
Let Tₐ = outside temperature (we'll use the average: 45°F).
By Newton's law: \[ \frac{dT}{dt} = k(T - 45) \] Step 2: General solution.
\[ \frac{dT}{T - 45} = k\,dt \] \[ \ln|T - 45| = kt + C \] \[ T(t) = 45 + ce^{kt} \] Step 3: Particular solution.
Choose t = 0 at 10 PM. T(0) = 70: \[ 70 = 45 + c \Rightarrow c = 25 \] \[ T(t) = 45 + 25e^{kt} \] Step 4: Determine k.
At t = 4 (2 AM), T(4) = 65: \[ 65 = 45 + 25e^{4k} \] \[ e^{4k} = 0.8 \] \[ k = \frac{1}{4}\ln(0.8) = -0.0558 \] \[ T(t) = 45 + 25e^{-0.0558t} \] Step 5: Answer.
At t = 8 (6 AM): \[ T(8) = 45 + 25e^{-0.0558 \times 8} = 45 + 25(0.639) = \boxed{60.98^\circ\text{F}} \]
📖 Example 4: Cooling of a Cake

Problem: A cake is at 300°F when it leaves the oven. Ten minutes later, it is 200°F. The room temperature is 60°F. When will the cake be practically at room temperature (say, 61°F)?

Solution:
Let T(t) = temperature of the cake at time t (in minutes).
Room temperature Tₐ = 60°F.
By Newton's law: \[ \frac{dT}{dt} = k(T - 60) \] \[ T(t) = 60 + ce^{kt} \] T(0) = 300: 300 = 60 + c ⇒ c = 240
\[ T(t) = 60 + 240e^{kt} \] T(10) = 200: \[ 200 = 60 + 240e^{10k} \] \[ e^{10k} = \frac{140}{240} = \frac{7}{12} \] \[ k = \frac{1}{10}\ln\left(\frac{7}{12}\right) = -0.0539 \] \[ T(t) = 60 + 240e^{-0.0539t} \] For T = 61°F: \[ 61 = 60 + 240e^{-0.0539t} \] \[ e^{-0.0539t} = \frac{1}{240} \] \[ t = \frac{\ln(240)}{0.0539} = \frac{5.4806}{0.0539} \approx \boxed{101.7 \text{ minutes}} \]
💧 Application 3: Torricelli's Law (Leaking Tank)
Torricelli's Law:
Under the influence of gravity, the outflow velocity from a hole in a tank is: \[ v(t) = 0.600\sqrt{2gh(t)} \] where h(t) is the height of water above the hole, and g = 980 cm/sec² = 32.17 ft/sec².

Model: \[ \frac{dh}{dt} = -26.56\frac{A}{B}\sqrt{h} \] where A = area of hole, B = cross-sectional area of tank.
📖 Example 5: Leaking Cylindrical Tank (Kreyszig Example 7, p. 16)

Problem: A cylindrical tank of diameter 2 m has a hole of diameter 1 cm at the bottom. The initial height of water is 2.25 m. Find the height h(t) at any time and determine when the tank will be empty.

Solution:
Step 1: Setting up the model.
A = π(0.005)² = 7.854 × 10⁻⁵ m²
B = π(1)² = π m²
A/B = 7.854 × 10⁻⁵ / π = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵
26.56 × (A/B) = 26.56 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.000664
\[ \frac{dh}{dt} = -0.000664\sqrt{h} \] Step 2: General solution.
\[ \frac{dh}{\sqrt{h}} = -0.000664\,dt \] \[ 2\sqrt{h} = C - 0.000664t \] \[ h(t) = \left(C - 0.000332t\right)^2 \] Step 3: Particular solution.
h(0) = 2.25 m = 225 cm ⇒ C = 15.00
\[ h(t) = (15.00 - 0.000332t)^2 \quad \text{(h in cm, t in seconds)} \] Step 4: Tank empty.
h(t) = 0 when: \[ 15.00 - 0.000332t = 0 \] \[ t = \frac{15.00}{0.000332} = \boxed{45,181 \text{ sec} = 12.6 \text{ hours}} \]
✏️ Practice Problem
Problem: A tank contains 500 L of brine with 40 kg of salt dissolved. Brine containing 0.2 kg/L of salt flows in at 5 L/min. The mixture is kept uniform and flows out at 5 L/min. Find the amount of salt in the tank at time t. When will the salt content reach 95% of its limiting value? CO1
✅ Solution:

Step 1: Setting up the model.
Let y(t) = amount of salt in the tank at time t (kg).
Volume of tank = 500 L (constant because inflow = outflow).
Inflow: 0.2 kg/L × 5 L/min = 1.0 kg/min
Outflow: (y/500) × 5 = y/100 kg/min

\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = 1.0 - \frac{y}{100} = -\frac{1}{100}(y - 100) \] Initial condition: y(0) = 40

Step 2: Solve.
\[ \frac{dy}{y - 100} = -\frac{1}{100}\,dt \] \[ \ln|y - 100| = -\frac{t}{100} + C \] \[ y - 100 = ce^{-t/100} \] y(0) = 40: 40 - 100 = c ⇒ c = -60
\[ \boxed{y(t) = 100 - 60e^{-t/100}} \] Step 3: Limiting value.
As t → ∞, y(t) → 100 kg.
95% of limiting value = 95 kg.
\[ 95 = 100 - 60e^{-t/100} \] \[ 60e^{-t/100} = 5 \] \[ e^{-t/100} = \frac{1}{12} \] \[ t = 100\ln(12) \approx \boxed{248.5 \text{ minutes} \approx 4.14 \text{ hours}} \]
⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid
MistakeCorrection
Forgetting the constant of integration CAlways add +C immediately after integration. If you add it later, you may get a wrong solution!
Confusing inflow and outflow rates/concentrationsCarefully identify: Inflow rate × Inflow concentration gives inflow of substance. Outflow rate × (amount/volume) gives outflow.
Using wrong sign in Newton's lawFor cooling: dT/dt = k(T - Tₐ) with k < 0. For heating: k > 0.
Forgetting units in Torricelli's lawConsistent units are essential! Use cm for g, meters for MKS.
📋 Quick Summary
  • Mixing Problem: y' = Inflow − Outflow, where Outflow = (y/V) × outflow rate
  • Newton's Law of Cooling: dT/dt = k(T − Tₐ), solution T(t) = Tₐ + (T₀ − Tₐ)e^(kt)
  • Torricelli's Law: dh/dt = −26.56(A/B)√h, solution h(t) = (√h₀ − 13.28(A/B)t)²
  • Modeling Steps: Set up model → Solve mathematically → Interpret physically
🕵️‍♂️ Case File #2026-MATH-08: The Alibi Clock 🔴 FORENSICS

🕵️ Monday Morning, 9:58 AM.

You walk up to your professor's office. The door is slightly ajar. You push it open, step inside, and freeze—your professor is lying motionless on the carpet.

The lead detective turns to you: "CCTV shows you entering this wing at 9:55 AM. No one else has passed through since 7:30 AM. Unless you can prove where you were the exact minute the crime occurred, you are our primary suspect."

You need an alibi. But an alibi is useless unless you know the precise time of death.

📊 Forensic Data:
  • 🕐 10:00 AM: Body temperature = 30°C
  • 🕐 11:00 AM: Body temperature = 28°C
  • 🌡️ Room Temperature (Tₐ): 24°C (constant)
  • ❤️ Normal Living Body Temperature: 37°C
💡 Key Insight: Thermal cooling obeys Newton's Law of Cooling. If you can solve the ODE, you can calculate the exact time of death and clear your name!
🔍 Step 1: Formulating the Differential Equation

By Newton's Law of Cooling: \[ \frac{dT}{dt} = k(T - 24) \] where:
  • T(t) = Body temperature at time t (hours)
  • t = 0 corresponds to 10:00 AM
  • Tₐ = 24°C
  • k = cooling constant (k < 0)
🔍 Step 2: General Solution

Separate variables: \[ \frac{dT}{T - 24} = k\,dt \] Integrate: \[ \ln|T - 24| = kt + C_1 \] \[ T(t) = 24 + Ce^{kt} \quad \text{— (Equation 1)} \]
🔍 Step 3: Determine C

Using T(0) = 30°C: \[ 30 = 24 + C \Rightarrow C = 6 \] \[ T(t) = 24 + 6e^{kt} \quad \text{— (Equation 2)} \]
🔍 Step 4: Determine k

Using T(1) = 28°C: \[ 28 = 24 + 6e^{k} \] \[ e^{k} = \frac{2}{3} \] \[ k = \ln\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) \approx -0.4055 \] \[ T(t) = 24 + 6e^{-0.4055t} \quad \text{— (Equation 3)} \]
🔍 Step 5: Calculate Time of Death (t_d)

At death, T(t_d) = 37°C: \[ 37 = 24 + 6e^{-0.4055t_d} \] \[ e^{-0.4055t_d} = \frac{13}{6} \] \[ -0.4055t_d = \ln\left(\frac{13}{6}\right) \approx 0.77319 \] \[ t_d = \frac{0.77319}{-0.4055} \approx -1.9068 \text{ hours} \] \[ 0.9068 \times 60 \approx 54 \text{ minutes} \] \[ \boxed{t_d \approx -1 \text{ hour } 54 \text{ minutes}} \]
🔎 Forensic Conclusion:

Since t = 0 is 10:00 AM, t_d = -1h 54m means death occurred: \[ \text{10:00 AM} - \text{1 hour 54 minutes} = \boxed{\mathbf{8:06 \text{ AM}}} \]
✅ Case Outcome: You show the police your cafeteria receipt and library log proving you were on the other side of campus from 7:45 AM to 9:30 AM. Thanks to Newton's Law of Cooling and separation of variables, your name is cleared!
