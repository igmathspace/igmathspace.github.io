10.10 Logical Implication

What does “logical implication” really mean? In everyday language, when we say “if it rains, the ground gets wet”, we mean the second fact follows from the first. In logic, we make this precise: \( P \) logically implies \( Q \) when there is no possible situation (no row of the truth table) in which \( P \) is true and \( Q \) is false. Logical implication is about the form of the statements, not their subject matter.

A proposition \( P(p, q, \dots) \) is said to logically imply a proposition \( Q(p, q, \dots) \), written \[ P(p, q, \dots) \Rightarrow Q(p, q, \dots) \] if \( Q(p, q, \dots) \) is true whenever \( P(p, q, \dots) \) is true.

Example 10.8. We claim that \( p \) logically implies \( p \lor q \). For consider the truth table in Fig. 10-11. Observe that \( p \) is true in Cases (rows) 1 and 2, and in these cases \( p \lor q \) is also true. Thus \( p \Rightarrow p \lor q \).

Real-life illustration. Let \( p \) be “It is raining” and \( q \) be “It is cloudy”. Then \( p \lor q \) is “It is raining or it is cloudy”. If it is indeed raining, then “It is raining or it is cloudy” is automatically true — it does not matter whether it is cloudy. So “It is raining” logically implies “It is raining or it is cloudy”. This is the pattern behind the logical rule called addition (or or-introduction): from any statement \( P \), infer \( P \lor Q \).

Now if \( Q(p, q, \dots) \) is true whenever \( P(p, q, \dots) \) is true, then the argument \[ P(p, q, \dots) \vdash Q(p, q, \dots) \] is valid; and conversely. Furthermore, the argument \( P \vdash Q \) is valid if and only if the conditional statement \( P \rightarrow Q \) is always true, i.e., a tautology. We state this result formally.

Theorem 10.4. For any propositions \( P(p, q, \dots) \) and \( Q(p, q, \dots) \) the following three statements are equivalent:
  • (i) \( P(p, q, \dots) \) logically implies \( Q(p, q, \dots) \).
  • (ii) The argument \( P(p, q, \dots) \vdash Q(p, q, \dots) \) is valid.
  • (iii) The proposition \( P(p, q, \dots) \rightarrow Q(p, q, \dots) \) is a tautology.
Why the theorem matters. The theorem gives three equivalent ways to check the same thing — semantic (truth tables), syntactic (valid arguments), and truth-table (tautology). For instance, to check whether “If a number is divisible by 4, then it is even” is a valid implication, we can reason directly, test the argument form, or build a truth table. All three routes lead to the same verdict.

We note that some logicians and many texts use the word “implies” in the same sense as we use “logically implies”, and so they distinguish between “implies” and “if . . . then”. These two distinct concepts are, of course, intimately related as seen in the above theorem.

10.11 Propositional Functions, Quantifiers

From sentences to conditions. A proposition is a sentence that is either true or false, like “7 is prime” or “Paris is in France”. But many sentences contain a variable: “\( x \) is prime”, “\( x + 2 > 7 \)”. These are propositional functions — they become propositions only after a value is substituted for \( x \). The truth set collects all values from the domain that make the condition true.

Let \( A \) be a given set. A propositional function (or an open sentence or condition) defined on \( A \) is an expression \[ p(x) \] which has the property that \( p(a) \) is true or false for each \( a \in A \). The set \( A \) is called the domain of \( p(x) \), and the set \( T_p \) of all elements of \( A \) for which \( p(a) \) is true is called the truth set of \( p(x) \). In other words, \[ T_p = \{\, x : x \in A,\ p(x) \text{ is true} \,\} \quad \text{or} \quad T_p = \{\, x : p(x) \,\} \]

Example 10.9. Find the truth set \( T_p \) of each propositional function \( p(x) \) defined on \( P = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\} \).
  • (a) \( p(x) \): “\( x + 2 > 7 \)”. \[ T_p = \{6, 7, 8, \dots\} \]
  • (b) \( p(x) \): “\( x + 5 < 3 \)”. \[ T_p = \emptyset \]
  • (c) \( p(x) \): “\( x + 5 > 1 \)”. \[ T_p = P \]
Real-life examples of propositional functions.
  • On all people, “\( x \) has a birthday in July” — truth set = people born in July.
  • On reals, “\( x^2 = 4 \)” — truth set = \( \{-2, 2\} \).
  • On positive integers, “\( x \) is even and \( x < 10 \)” — truth set = \( \{2, 4, 6, 8\} \).
Remark. \( p(x) \) could be true for all \( x \in A \), for some \( x \in A \), or for no \( x \in A \).

Universal Quantifier

Let \( p(x) \) be a propositional function defined on \( A \). The expression \[ (\forall x \in A)\,p(x) \quad \text{or} \quad \forall x\, p(x) \qquad (10.1) \] reads “For every \( x \) in \( A \), \( p(x) \) is a true statement”. The symbol \( \forall \) is called the universal quantifier. Statement (10.1) is equivalent to \[ T_p = \{\, x : x \in A,\ p(x) \,\} = A \qquad (10.2) \] that is, the truth set of \( p(x) \) is the entire set \( A \).

Real statement with a universal quantifier. “Every human being has a heart.” With \( H \) the set of humans and \( h(x) \): “\( x \) has a heart”, this is \( \forall x \in H,\, h(x) \). It is true iff no human lacks a heart — i.e., iff the truth set of \( h(x) \) is all of \( H \).

\( Q_1 \): If \( \{x : x \in A, p(x)\} = A \) then \( \forall x\, p(x) \) is true; otherwise, \( \forall x\, p(x) \) is false.

Example 10.10.
  • (a) \( (\forall n \in P)\,(n + 4 > 3) \) is true since \( \{\, n : n + 4 > 3 \,\} = P \).
  • (b) \( (\forall n \in P)\,(n + 2 > 8) \) is false since \( \{\, n : n + 2 > 8 \,\} = \{7, 8, \dots\} \neq P \).
  • (c) \( \bigcap (A_i : i \in I) = \{\, x : \forall i \in I,\ x \in A_i \,\} \).

Existential Quantifier

The expression \[ (\exists x \in A)\,p(x) \quad \text{or} \quad \exists x\, p(x) \qquad (10.3) \] reads “There exists an \( x \) in \( A \) such that \( p(x) \) is true”. The symbol \( \exists \) is called the existential quantifier. Statement (10.3) is equivalent to \[ T_p = \{\, x : x \in A,\ p(x) \,\} \neq \emptyset \qquad (10.4) \] i.e., the truth set of \( p(x) \) is not empty.

Real statement with an existential quantifier. “Some students in this class speak French.” With \( C \) the set of students and \( F(x) \): “\( x \) speaks French”, this is \( \exists x \in C,\, F(x) \). It is true iff at least one student speaks French. Another example: “There is a prime number greater than 100” — true (e.g., 101). A false one: “There is a real number whose square is negative.”

\( Q_2 \): If \( \{x : p(x)\} \neq \emptyset \) then \( \exists x\, p(x) \) is true; otherwise, \( \exists x\, p(x) \) is false.

Example 10.11.
  • (a) \( (\exists n \in P)\,(n + 4 < 7) \) is true since \( \{\, n : n + 4 < 7 \,\} = \{1, 2\} \neq \emptyset \).
  • (b) \( (\exists n \in P)\,(n + 6 < 4) \) is false since \( \{\, n : n + 6 < 4 \,\} = \emptyset \).
  • (c) \( \bigcup (A_i : i \in I) = \{\, x : \exists i \in I,\ x \in A_i \,\} \).

Notation

Let \( A = \{2, 3, 5\} \) and \( p(x) \): “\( x \) is prime”. Then

\( p(2) \land p(3) \land p(5) \equiv \forall a \in A,\, p(a) \)

\( p(2) \lor p(3) \lor p(5) \equiv \exists a \in A,\, p(a) \)

Remark: If \( A \) is infinite, the conjunction form cannot be written out, but the quantified form always can.

10.12 Negation of Quantified Statements

Negating a quantified statement is not always intuitive. The negation of “All students are hardworking” is not “No student is hardworking”. It is “Some student is not hardworking”. The quantifier flips and the inner condition is negated. This is the content of DeMorgan's laws for quantifiers.

Consider the statement: “All math majors are male”. Its negation is either of the following equivalent statements:

  • “It is not the case that all math majors are male”
  • “There exists at least one math major who is a female (not male)”

Symbolically, using \( M \) for the set of math majors and \( p(x) \): “\( x \) is male”, \[ \neg (\forall x \in M)\, p(x) \equiv (\exists x \in M)\, \neg p(x) \]

Theorem 10.5 (DeMorgan). \( \neg (\forall x \in A)\, p(x) \equiv (\exists x \in A)\, \neg p(x) \).

In other words, the following two statements are equivalent:

  1. It is not true that, for all \( a \in A \), \( p(a) \) is true.
  2. There exists an \( a \in A \) such that \( p(a) \) is false.
Theorem 10.6 (DeMorgan). \( \neg (\exists x \in A)\, p(x) \equiv (\forall x \in A)\, \neg p(x) \).

That is, the following two statements are equivalent:

  1. It is not true that for some \( a \in A \), \( p(a) \) is true.
  2. For all \( a \in A \), \( p(a) \) is false.
Real statements and their negations.
  • “All birds can fly.” Negation: “Some bird cannot fly.” (Ostriches, penguins.)
  • “Some prime numbers are even.” Negation: “No prime number is even.”
  • “Every student in the class passed.” Negation: “At least one student did not pass.”
  • “There exists a real \( x \) with \( x^2 = -1 \).” Negation: “For every real \( x \), \( x^2 \neq -1 \).”
Example 10.12.
  • (a) “For all positive integers \( n \), \( n + 2 > 8 \)” vs. “There exists a positive integer \( n \) with \( n + 2 \neq 8 \)”.
  • (b) “There exists a college student who is 60” vs. “Every college student is not 60”.
Remark. In terms of truth sets: \( \neg p(x) \) is the complement of \( p(x) \); \( p(x) \land q(x) \) is the intersection; \( p(x) \lor q(x) \) is the union. DeMorgan's laws hold: \[ \neg (p(x) \land q(x)) \equiv \neg p(x) \lor \neg q(x) \] \[ \neg (p(x) \lor q(x)) \equiv \neg p(x) \land \neg q(x) \]

Counterexample

To show \( \forall x,\, p(x) \) is false, it suffices to produce one \( x_0 \) with \( p(x_0) \) false. Such an \( x_0 \) is a counterexample.

Counterexamples in action. To disprove “All prime numbers are odd”, produce 2. To disprove “Every student scored above 50”, find one student who scored 50 or below. A single counterexample destroys a universal statement.
Example 10.13.
  • (a) \( \forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, |x| \neq 0 \) is false; 0 is a counterexample.
  • (b) \( \forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, x^2 \geq x \) is false; \( 1/2 \) is a counterexample.
  • (c) \( \forall x \in \mathbb{P},\, x^2 \geq x \) is true (no counterexample).

Propositional Functions with More than One Variable

A propositional function \( p(x_1, \dots, x_n) \) on \( A_1 \times \cdots \times A_n \) becomes a statement when quantified for each variable. For example, \[ x + 2y + 3z < 18 \] is a propositional function on \( \mathbb{P}^3 \).

Example 10.14. Let \( B = \{1, \dots, 9\} \) and \( p(x,y) \): “\( x + y = 10 \)”.
  • (a) \( \forall x \exists y,\, p(x,y) \) — true (choose \( y = 10 - x \)).
  • (b) \( \exists y \forall x,\, p(x,y) \) — false (no single \( y \) works for all \( x \)).

Warning! Order of quantifiers matters.

Order of quantifiers — real-life comparison.
  • “Everyone has a mother” — \( \forall x \exists y \). True.
  • “There is a mother of everyone” — \( \exists y \forall x \). False.

Negating Multi-Variable Statements

Apply DeMorgan successively, flipping each quantifier: \[ \neg[\forall x \exists y \exists z,\, p(x,y,z)] \equiv \exists x \forall y \forall z,\, \neg p(x,y,z) \]

Example 10.15.
  • (a) “Every student has at least one course where the lecturer is a TA” → negation: “There is a student such that in every course the lecturer is not a TA”.
  • (b) \( L \) is the limit of \( (a_n) \) iff \[ \forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists n_0,\ \forall n > n_0,\ |a_n - L| < \varepsilon \] Negation: \[ \exists \varepsilon > 0,\ \forall n_0,\ \exists n > n_0,\ |a_n - L| \ge \varepsilon \]
